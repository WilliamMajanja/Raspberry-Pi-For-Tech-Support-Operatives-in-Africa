# Mine Action and Explosive-Hazard Support

This chapter applies peace-support and humanitarian mine-action concepts to **technical information support**. It does not train readers to locate, identify, move, mark, render safe, or clear mines, unexploded ordnance (UXO), explosive remnants of war (ERW), or improvised explosive devices (IEDs). Those are hazardous activities for appropriately trained and accredited specialists working under the responsible national authority and applicable standards.

## 1. Why mine action matters to technical support

Explosive ordnance can threaten civilians, returnees, humanitarian staff, peacekeepers, livelihoods, access to land, and public infrastructure long after active conflict has ended. Mine action is broader than clearance. The IMAS framework describes complementary work including explosive-ordnance risk education (EORE), survey and land release, victim assistance, stockpile destruction, and advocacy.

The United Nations Security Council’s [resolution 2365 (2017)](https://docs.un.org/en/S/RES/2365(2017)) recognized mine action’s role in civilian protection and peacebuilding and called for attention to risk education, victim assistance, relevant standards, coordination, and planning. Resolution 1325 (2000) also calls attention to gender considerations in mine clearance and mine awareness programmes.

## 2. High-impact field practices

The practices below are **high-impact, standards-based methods**: they make decisions more evidence-based, inclusive, and sustainable without asking unqualified staff to handle explosive hazards. Their value comes from a disciplined process and trained people, not from a shortcut or a new explosive-ordnance procedure.

The transformative shift is from clearance-only thinking to a coordinated system: evidence-led land release, risk education shaped and tested with affected communities, survivor-centered referral, and information that supports accountable decisions. These practices can reduce preventable exposure and improve how scarce specialist capacity is prioritized; they do not replace trained survey, clearance, or EOD teams.

### A. Start with non-technical survey and evidence quality

IMAS 08.10 describes non-technical survey (NTS) as collection and analysis of information without technical interventions, to help determine where explosive-ordnance contamination is present or absent and inform land-release decisions. It may use historical records, reports, field visits, and consultation with affected communities. NTS is not a clearance operation and does not authorize a support worker to approach suspicious items.

For a technical support operative maintaining a survey or reporting system:

- preserve the source and date of every record; distinguish observation, hearsay, and verified findings;
- keep uncertainty and confidence visible instead of silently converting reports into confirmed facts;
- use approved forms, standardized fields, time and map references, versioned records, and audit trails;
- protect raw records and coordinate corrections with the accredited survey team or national mine-action authority;
- support secure backups and offline operation where connectivity is intermittent, then use an approved reconciliation process;
- do not publicly expose hazard coordinates, reporter identities, or access routes.

NTS requires competent, trained, and in the relevant context accredited survey personnel. Decisions to cancel, reduce, or clear land belong to authorized mine-action decision-makers under the applicable national standards and “all reasonable effort” requirements.

### B. Make risk education local, inclusive, and feedback-driven

EORE should be based on local needs and changing risk patterns, and designed with the intended communities. Messages may need adaptation for language, age, gender, disability, literacy, displacement, and local communication channels. Do not circulate an unverified safety poster or translate a warning mechanically and assume it is understood.

Technical support can help by:

- maintaining approved, dated versions of public safety messages;
- supporting authorized radio, print, mobile, or offline distribution plans;
- tracking reach and feedback without collecting unnecessary identity data;
- ensuring that formats are usable with low bandwidth, assistive technology, and common local devices;
- routing reports and questions to designated mine-action professionals.

If a person encounters a suspected explosive item, the support worker should not approach, touch, photograph at close range, collect components, or attempt identification. Follow the locally approved public-safety guidance, move away from danger without approaching the item, and notify the designated emergency or mine-action authority through the established channel.

### C. Treat victim assistance as a coordinated, long-term responsibility

IMAS 13.10 and UNMAS guidance frame victim assistance as a rights-based, multi-sector effort involving access to emergency and continuing health care, rehabilitation, psychosocial support, education, social and economic inclusion, and relevant public services. Mine-action technical teams can help identify needs and connect people with established services; they do not replace health, rehabilitation, or social-protection providers.

Information systems should use the approved referral pathway, collect only necessary information, respect consent and confidentiality, and include survivor organizations and persons with disabilities in design and review. Data disaggregation by sex, age, disability, and other locally relevant categories can expose gaps, but only collect and share it when authorized, necessary, and protected from re-identification or stigma.

### D. Coordinate nationally and across sectors

Mine action involves affected-state authorities, UNMAS, humanitarian organizations, civil society, technical operators, and communities. Information systems should make ownership, data stewardship, access, and handover responsibilities explicit. Software must not create a parallel “hazard authority” or let a user mark land as safe without the prescribed assessment and approval.

### E. Use technology as a support layer—not a substitute for expertise

A Raspberry Pi or other low-cost computer may support an offline reference library, local reporting, inventory, training, or synchronization workflow. Before field use, assess physical security, power, storage durability, backup and restore, user access, data retention, secure transport, and what happens when the device is lost. Verify data formats and interoperability with the authorized mine-action information-management system.

Do not use generic image recognition, consumer GPS, crowdsourced reports, or an AI model to classify suspected ordnance or recommend a route through a suspected hazardous area. Such decisions require trained personnel and authoritative survey evidence.

## 3. A support workflow for mine-action information

The following workflow adapts IMAS information- and risk-management principles to ordinary technical support. The National Mine Action Authority (NMAA) or designated coordination body owns the operational rules and data authority; confirm local roles before configuring a system.

1. **Agree the information need.** Ask the responsible operations and information-management staff what decision the data supports, who provides it, who validates it, who approves sharing, and when it must be available.
2. **Use approved schemas and forms.** Record source, collection time, precision, version, and appropriate uncertainty. Do not invent fields or silently reinterpret a surveyor’s finding.
3. **Protect community data.** Explain why information is being collected, obtain consent where required, minimize personal identifiers, respect confidentiality, and include community members who may otherwise be excluded.
4. **Validate with the responsible role.** Check completeness and consistency with designated information-management and operations staff. Correct errors through the approved revision process; preserve the original record and audit trail.
5. **Synchronize safely.** For offline use, document how records will be reconciled and conflicts resolved. Protect portable media and do not post raw records or hazard maps in public systems.
6. **Back up and test recovery.** Back up authorized data, restrict access to copies, and test restoration without overwriting live information.
7. **Review and improve.** Track data-quality problems, context changes, user feedback, and EORE message versions. Route operational decisions and risk acceptance to authorized decision-makers.

IMAS 05.10 assigns national information-management ownership and defines operational and information-management responsibilities. A support operative should not independently approve operational records, determine clearance priorities, authorize data sharing, or declare land safe. Do not promise that collecting a report will raise an area’s priority for clearance.

### EORE material release checklist for support staff

Technical support may distribute **approved** risk-education material but should not invent or alter explosive-ordnance safety messaging. Before preparing an offline pack, kiosk, SMS, web page, or shared device, check that:

- the responsible authority or qualified EORE operator approved the exact content and version;
- language, reading level, disability access, age, and local communication channels were considered by qualified staff;
- the target group and safe referral/reporting route are present and current;
- the material can be withdrawn or replaced quickly when risks or official instructions change; and
- use and feedback can be monitored without exposing personal data or sensitive hazard locations.

IMAS 12.10 calls for needs assessment, community involvement, message/material testing, monitoring, and review. The technical-support role is version control, distribution, uptime, accessibility, and privacy—not authoring unapproved safety advice.

## 4. Explosive-hazard encounters and role boundaries

If support work reveals a possible explosive hazard, stop the affected task, avoid further approach, warn people only in the manner specified by local procedures, and contact the designated authority. Do not:

- touch, move, open, dismantle, test, mark, or photograph an item at close range;
- ask untrained community members to inspect or identify it;
- publish precise hazard locations on an open map or public ticket;
- circulate unverified “safe route” advice;
- treat a generic online description or model-generated result as an identification.

IMAS, national mine-action standards, and mission procedures take precedence. The technical support role is to protect systems, records, communications, and authorized workflows.
