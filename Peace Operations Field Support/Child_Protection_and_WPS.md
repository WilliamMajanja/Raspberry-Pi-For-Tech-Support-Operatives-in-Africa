# Child Protection and Women, Peace and Security

This overview is intended for **technical support operatives** who may support systems used by peace-support or humanitarian teams. It is not a substitute for child-protection, gender, protection-of-civilians, or mission-specific training. Follow the responsible organization’s safeguarding policies and the designated child-protection, gender, human-rights, and operational focal points.

## 1. Child protection in armed conflict

The UN Security Council’s children-and-armed-conflict agenda recognizes six grave violations against children:

1. recruitment and use of children by armed forces or armed groups;
2. killing and maiming;
3. rape and other forms of sexual violence;
4. abduction;
5. attacks on schools and hospitals; and
6. denial of humanitarian access.

The [Office of the Special Representative for Children and Armed Conflict](https://childrenandarmedconflict.un.org/en/six-grave-violations/) explains the mandate and monitoring/reporting mechanism. Resolution 1612 (2005) established the monitoring and reporting mechanism and Security Council Working Group. Resolution 2764 (2024) stresses sustainable child-protection capacity, accountability, coordination, and continuity during peace-operation transitions.

### What technical teams should do

- Protect case and incident data with strict role-based access, approved retention, secure transfer, and auditability.
- Do not put names, images, exact locations, allegations, or sensitive case details in public bug trackers, group chats, screenshots, or unencrypted removable media.
- Do not interview, photograph, identify, or collect personal details from a child as part of ordinary IT troubleshooting. Refer concerns through the approved child-protection focal point.
- Design forms and systems to collect the minimum necessary information, avoid exposing a child’s identity, and work with specialists on safeguarding and consent.
- Maintain availability and safe handover of approved services during staff or mission transitions; protect credentials and audit trails.
- Ensure that digital learning or case-support systems do not expose children to surveillance, stigmatization, or unsafe contact.

UN military child-protection training is mission-specific. POTI’s [Child Protection for UN Military](https://www.peaceopstraining.org/courses/child-protection-en-2025/) course describes scenario-based training, mandate awareness, actor coordination, and the roles of military personnel. Its training materials are sourced from the UN Peacekeeping Resource Hub under a revocable license; this book summarizes only the public course description and does not reproduce its materials or scenarios.

## 2. Women, Peace and Security (WPS)

The WPS agenda is commonly organized around **participation, prevention, protection, and relief/recovery**. It recognizes women’s meaningful participation in peace and security decisions, prevention of conflict-related sexual violence, protection of rights, and gender-responsive relief and recovery. The agenda is not limited to women as victims; it also recognizes women’s leadership and agency in prevention, mediation, peacebuilding, and community resilience.

The UN Women [Global Norms and Standards page](https://www.unwomen.org/en/what-we-do/peace-and-security/global-norms-and-standards) provides summaries of the ten core thematic WPS Security Council resolutions. UN Peacemaker also lists the [WPS Security Council resolutions](https://peacemaker.un.org/en/thematic-areas/women-peace-security/normative-frameworks/security-council-resolutions).

### Applying WPS principles to technical support

- Consult affected users—including women-led organizations and groups often excluded from design—before selecting devices, reporting channels, access controls, or training formats.
- Check who can access, operate, charge, repair, and safely use each tool; identify barriers related to language, literacy, disability, safety, cost, connectivity, and social norms.
- Do not collect gender, age, location, or incident data “just in case.” Collect only what is authorized and needed; restrict access and assess re-identification or retaliation risks.
- Provide confidential and accessible reporting routes; do not promise anonymity unless the technical design and operational procedure can support it.
- Make training materials usable offline and in locally appropriate formats where needed, while respecting data protection and consent.
- Include safe handover so that data, contact lists, or service access are not lost when a project or mission changes.

POTI’s [Women, Peace and Security in Africa](https://www.peaceopstraining.org/courses/wps-africa-en-2021/) course was developed by UN Women in collaboration with POTI. It covers implementation of UNSCR 1325 and subsequent resolutions, Africa-specific priorities, and roles for regional and subregional organizations. It is a course reference, not an endorsement by the course authors of this guide.

## 3. Resolution and mandate awareness

Security Council resolutions are not interchangeable checklists. Their operative paragraphs, addressees, scope, and legal effect depend on the exact text and context. A country or mission mandate may add requirements not captured in this thematic overview. Read the current mandate and full resolution text before configuring a system or deciding what information may be collected.

See the [selected resolution reference](Security_Council_Resolution_Reference.md), the UN Security Council’s [official resolution archive](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en/content/resolutions-0), and its [WPS dashboard](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en/content/women-and-peace-and-security-dashboard).
