# Researching Public Harm and Corporate Accountability

Public-interest Informatics asks how evidence, technology, institutions, and economic incentives affect people. This chapter offers a reproducible way to investigate alleged harm without overstating what the evidence proves. It is not legal or medical advice, and it does not declare that every organization in an industry behaves alike.

## 1. Turn a broad claim into a testable question

“Companies are harming people” is too broad to evaluate. Specify:

- the named organization, product, process, or decision;
- the affected population and place;
- the period and relevant version or exposure;
- the alleged harm and how it was measured;
- the decision or duty that is in question;
- what evidence would support, weaken, or falsify the claim.

Separate five questions that are often conflated:

1. **Hazard:** Can an agent or practice cause a kind of harm under some conditions?
2. **Exposure:** Did people encounter it, through which route, at what level and duration?
3. **Risk/impact:** What is the likelihood and severity under the observed conditions, compared with an appropriate alternative?
4. **Causation:** Does the evidence show that this exposure contributed to the observed outcome, accounting for other explanations?
5. **Responsibility and intent:** What did a named decision-maker know, decide, disclose, or fail to do, and what standard or law applied?

Evidence supporting one question does not automatically establish the next. An animal toxicology result, a hazard classification, a cohort association, a regulator’s risk assessment, an internal corporate memo, an admission, and a court judgment are different evidence types.

## 2. Use an evidence ledger

Keep a source ledger while researching. For each item, record author/institution, title, publication date, jurisdiction, source type, URL or archive, question addressed, methods and population, result, uncertainty, funding/conflict disclosure, and the exact claim it supports. Keep quotations short and attributed; prefer original wording only where necessary, otherwise paraphrase and cite.

### Practical source hierarchy

Use the source best suited to the question rather than a single universal ranking:

- **Primary records:** court judgments, plea agreements, regulator orders, company filings, inspection reports, product labels, and original datasets.
- **Research evidence:** peer-reviewed studies, systematic reviews, evidence assessments, protocols, and replication studies.
- **Public authorities:** technical guidance, surveillance, standards, and risk assessments from competent agencies.
- **Affected-community evidence:** testimony, community monitoring, union records, and local studies, handled with consent and protection from retaliation.
- **Investigative reporting and advocacy:** useful for leads and context; trace central factual claims to documents or interviews where possible.
- **Company statements and press releases:** evidence of what the company says, not independent proof that the claim is accurate.

Primary does not mean unbiased or complete. A court document may establish an admission or legal finding within its jurisdiction, not prove every public allegation. A peer-reviewed result can still have selection bias, measurement error, small samples, or limited generalizability. Record limitations instead of assigning a source a simplistic “true/false” label.

## 3. Separate association, causation, and intent

When evaluating a health or environmental claim, ask whether the data establish temporal order, how exposure was measured, what comparison group was used, how confounding and missing data were handled, whether results were replicated, and whether the effect size and uncertainty are reported. Distinguish a statistically detectable association from a causal effect, and a causal effect from proof about a particular company’s knowledge or intent.

For intent or organizational responsibility, relevant evidence may include internal communications, product testing, regulatory submissions, warnings, meeting records, policy decisions, audit trails, testimony, admissions, and judicial or administrative findings. A financial incentive can motivate inquiry; alone it proves neither the action nor the result.

## 4. Compare the right kind of cases

| Case type | What strong evidence can show | What it does not automatically show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A corporate plea or judgment | The specific admitted or adjudicated conduct, parties, jurisdiction, and legal counts | That every firm in the industry engaged in the same conduct |
| A regulator privacy order | The practices and legal obligations addressed in that order | That the same facts or legal finding apply to another platform |
| A hazard classification | Evidence that an agent may cause harm under some conditions | The risk at a particular dose, use pattern, or site |
| A field exposure study | Measured exposure/outcome in a defined population and time | Universal causation or transferability to an unstudied population |
| A community report | Local experience, observed events, and issues for investigation | A complete causal analysis without corroboration or context |

The public-interest chapter already cites specific examples: Purdue Pharma’s 2020 guilty pleas and admissions summarized by the US Department of Justice; the Federal Trade Commission’s Facebook privacy settlement and its Cambridge Analytica proceeding; and WHO’s global tobacco-health estimates. Describe each record narrowly, include dates and jurisdiction, and distinguish an agency charge, order, settlement, plea, and final judgment.

## 5. Evaluate agricultural and cancer claims without collapsing categories

“Genetically modified,” “pesticide,” “seed company,” and “cancer” are not interchangeable terms. Identify the crop and trait, active ingredient and formulation, use pattern, exposure route, dose, duration, population, measured outcome, and applicable regulator. A herbicide-tolerant trait does not itself specify a person’s herbicide exposure. A pesticide hazard classification is not a measurement of a particular worker’s exposure or risk.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) identifies carcinogenic hazards using its Monographs process. The US National Academies’ 2016 consensus report assessed particular commercial GE crops and evidence available at that time; it is not a universal conclusion about all future traits, farming practices, or products. National regulators may conduct exposure-specific risk assessments under different mandates. Cite the document and date, state what population/product it covers, and describe uncertainty.

An appropriate comparison may include conventional and GE varieties, different application practices, non-pesticide exposures, worker protection, environmental effects, farmer choice, and economic conditions. The comparison must match the question. Do not infer that every pesticide is harmless from the safety of a particular food, or that every GE crop causes cancer from a hazard evaluation of a particular chemical.

## 6. Build an accountable and ethical investigation

1. **Define the question and public interest.** Identify who needs the answer and what decision it could affect.
2. **Set a safe data plan.** Use public or synthetic data first. Do not collect health, genomic, child-protection, precise location, or whistleblower information without appropriate authority, consent/notification, safeguards, and a secure channel.
3. **Preserve provenance.** Keep source URL, access date, publication date, version, original file hash where useful, and collection method. A hash helps detect byte changes; it does not prove source authenticity.
4. **Corroborate.** Seek independent evidence and look for plausible alternative explanations, contradictory findings, and missing populations.
5. **Quantify and qualify.** Report denominators, absolute as well as relative comparisons where relevant, uncertainty, methods, and what was not measured.
6. **Distinguish assertion status.** Label allegation, reported claim, company response, regulator action, admission, scientific finding, and court judgment accurately.
7. **Protect people.** Minimize identity and location details, consider retaliation and re-identification, provide a correction channel, and consult affected communities safely.
8. **Publish reproducibly.** Cite durable sources, record versions and analysis decisions, avoid publishing restricted records, and document corrections and later evidence.

## 7. Printable source-review worksheet

```text
Question / claim being assessed:
Named actor, product or practice:
Population, place, and period:
Hazard / exposure / outcome:
What evidence would change the conclusion?

Source title, author, institution, date, jurisdiction:
Source type and version / archived copy:
Method, population, and denominator:
Result and uncertainty:
Funding / conflicts / limitations:
Exact narrow claim this source supports:
Contradictory or alternative evidence to check:
Personal or operational data risk:
Reviewer, date, and correction contact:
```

Do not include confidential source identities in an ordinary printed worksheet. Store sensitive material under the responsible organization’s protected process.
